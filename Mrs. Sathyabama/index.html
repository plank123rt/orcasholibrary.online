1.3 Reading the pattern together

The pattern is not a simple ankle-level peroneal nerve entrapment. A pure fibular-head compression would usually be one-sided and would spare the H-reflex. Here the abnormality is bilateral, is largely axonal (amplitude ↓ more than velocity), and sits alongside a bilateral H-reflex delay (which reflects the S1 afferent–efferent arc through the tibial nerve/soleus a different nerve entirely). Sensory fibres in both legs are completely normal. This combination motor-predominant, bilateral, axonal, with a proximal reflex-arc abnormality but preserved sensation is exactly why the neurologist labelled it a radiculoneuropathy rather than an isolated mononeuropathy: the lesion behaves as though it sits at or near the nerve root level (L4–S1 myotomes), i.e. at the spine, not just at the leg.

1.4 One honest caveat

The "Temp (°C)" field on every motor page was left blank. Peripheral nerve conduction velocity falls by roughly 1.5–2.4 m/s for every 1 °C drop in limb surface temperature. Because both CPN velocities (29.8 and 32.6 m/s) are meaningfully below the ~44 m/s a cool limb alone could explain, a real conduction abnormality is still highly likely but the treating neurologist should confirm limb temperature was controlled before the axonal/demyelinating split is finalised. This document treats the report's own conclusion (axonal radiculoneuropathy) as the working diagnosis.

2The physiology behind the numbers

Every physiotherapy instruction in §6 is anchored to one of the mechanisms below.

2.1 How a healthy motor axon conducts and why myelin matters

The resting/action potential of the axon membrane is governed by the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation:

V_m = (RT/F) · ln[ (P_K[K⁺]_o + P_Na[Na⁺]_o + P_Cl[Cl⁻]_i) / (P_K[K⁺]_i + P_Na[Na⁺]_i + P_Cl[Cl⁻]_o) ]

Depolarisation only regenerates at the nodes of Ranvier (myelin insulates between nodes), so the impulse "jumps" node-to-node saltatory conduction. Conduction velocity scales approximately with:

θ (conduction velocity) ∝ D / √(R_m · C_m) [D = internodal distance / fibre diameter]

measured directly in the lab as:

CV = Distance (mm) / [Proximal latency − Distal latency] (ms)

Applying this to the patient's left CPN data: CV = 350 mm / (16.38 − 4.63 ms) = 350/11.75 = 29.8 m/s matching the report exactly, roughly 30–35% slower than the ~44 m/s expected for this segment.

2.2 The causal cascade from root insult to clinical weakness and back pain

  1. Insult at/near the lumbosacral root or proximal course (mechanical/discogenic, compressive, ischaemic or metabolic) → focal segmental stress on motor axons serving L4–S1 myotomes, and irritation of the sinuvertebral/dorsal-ramus afferents that produce local back pain.
  2. Segmental demyelination at the stressed segment → internodal capacitance rises → each node-to-node jump takes longer → CV ↓ (this alone explains the slowed 29.8–32.6 m/s velocities).
  3. If sustained, the axon distal to the lesion undergoes Wallerian degeneration axoplasm/myelin distal to the injury fragment and are cleared by Schwann cells/macrophages, typically evident on NCS within 5–7 days of onset. The number of surviving, functioning motor axons falls.
  4. CMAP amplitude is a direct read-out of how many motor axons are still firing into the muscle:
    CMAP amplitude ∝ Σ (functioning motor units) × (average unit twitch force)
    Fewer functioning axons → lower CMAP (0.63/0.26 mV vs the >2 mV expected) → the "axonal" half of the diagnosis.
  5. Fewer motor units reaching tibialis anterior, EDL, EDB → reduced dorsiflexion/toe extension force → clinical foot-drop tendency and slap-foot gait.
  6. Unopposed, intact plantarflexors (gastro-soleus, tibial nerve confirmed normal) continue pulling the ankle into plantarflexion at rest → risk of secondary Achilles/gastrocnemius contracture.
  7. The H-reflex tests the Ia-afferent → spinal synapse → alpha-motor-efferent arc (an electrical ankle-jerk). Latency is prolonged (38.0/36.0 ms) but amplitude is normal the motor unit count in that arc is preserved, but conduction time through it is delayed. Because this arc runs through the tibial nerve (normal on direct testing), the delay most plausibly originates more proximally at the S1 nerve root reinforcing the "radiculo-" component.
  8. Reflex protective guarding of the lumbar paraspinals, altered pelvic tilt to offload the irritated root, and compensatory hip-hike/circumduction during the swing phase of gait (to clear the dropping foot) together increase asymmetric loading on the lumbar facets and discs this is the mechanical loop by which a foot-drop pattern can itself keep aggravating the very low back pain that is already present. Addressing only the ankle, or only the back, in isolation leaves this loop running.

2.3 Why physiotherapy can act on each of these steps

Step 2 (demyelination/CV): Neurodynamic (nerve-gliding) technique reduces mechanical tension/compression at the presumed root/entrapment site, supports local microcirculation, and creates a mechanical environment more favourable to remyelination.
Step 3–4 (axon loss/CMAP): Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) recruits the motor units that remain, slows disuse atrophy while regeneration occurs, and provides sensory feedback assisting cortical/spinal reorganisation.
Step 5 (voluntary weakness): Task-specific resistance training obeys Henneman's Size Principle low-threshold motor units are recruited first, larger ones as load increases so graded loading systematically re-trains the surviving motor pool.
Step 6 (contracture risk): Static/dynamic stretching and night positioning keep the gastro-soleus–Achilles complex at resting length while dorsiflexors are weak.
Step 8 (back-leg-foot loop): Lumbar mechanical unloading, core stabilisation and gait-pattern correction interrupt the compensatory loading loop, so the spine stops paying the price for the ankle's weakness and vice-versa.

2.4 Peripheral nerve regeneration the timescale that actually justifies 6 months (and why 12 weeks alone was too short)

Once an axon is disrupted, regrowth (assuming the axon tube/endoneurium is intact an axonotmesis-type injury, not a full transection) proceeds at roughly:

Regeneration rate ≈ 1–3 mm/day (the classical "~1 mm/day" Sunderland estimate)

This patient's suspected lesion sits at the lumbosacral root, and the target muscle (EDB, on the dorsum of the foot) is roughly 700–850 mm away along the nerve's course (root → lumbosacral plexus → sciatic → common peroneal → deep peroneal → EDB). Applying the regeneration equation:

Time to reinnervate ≈ Distance / Rate = 750 mm / (1 to 3 mm/day) ≈ 250 to 750 days (≈ 8 to 25 months)

Even at the fastest plausible biological rate, meaningful reinnervation of the distal foot muscles takes roughly 8 months which is precisely why a 12-week programme could only ever address symptoms and compensation, never the nerve itself. A 6-month (24-week) programme is the minimum realistic window to (a) see early objective change in CMAP amplitude/CV on a mid-programme NCS, and (b) convert whatever reinnervation has occurred into usable strength. If week-24 re-testing shows only partial change, this document explicitly recommends continuing into a 7th–12th month maintenance/advanced phase (§8) rather than discharging the patient.

3The back connection one root, three complaints

The referring history notes co-existing low back pain alongside the "? SPN" query. The electrodiagnostic pattern in §1.3 bilateral, axonal, motor-predominant, with a delayed proximal H-reflex arc localises to the same anatomical neighbourhood that low back pain of a lumbosacral origin comes from. This section makes that link explicit, because a programme that treats "the foot" and "the back" as two separate problems will under-treat both.

How to read each instruction below

Every instruction carries four boxes: Why (the physiological rationale, tied back to §2), How (exact technique/dosage for the physiotherapist to execute), Interpretation (what a good response should look like at re-check, and what a poor response implies), and, where relevant, Back–leg–foot link (how that specific exercise closes the loop described in §2.2/§3).

PHASE 1 Weeks 1–6: Protect the joint, unload the root, activate every surviving motor unit

Goal: settle root irritability and back pain, prevent ankle contracture and falls, and give every surviving CPN motor unit maximal activation input, without provoking either the spine or the peripheral nerve.

5.1 Lumbar mechanical assessment & directional-preference exercise

Assess once; then 8–10 reps, hourly during flare, 4–6×/day baseline
How: Perform a MacKenzie-style repeated-movement assessment (repeated lumbar extension in lying, then repeated flexion in sitting) to find whether symptoms centralise (move proximally, toward the spine) or peripheralise (move distally, toward the leg) with a given direction. Prescribe repeated low-load movement in whichever direction centralises symptoms; avoid the direction that peripheralises them.
Why: Reducing mechanical loading in the direction that peripheralises symptoms lowers compressive/inflammatory stress on the same L4–S1 root that is producing the abnormal CPN motor conduction (§1.3, §2.2 step 1) this is the single highest-yield early intervention because it addresses the presumed source, not just the downstream nerve.
Interpretation: Centralisation within the first 1–2 weeks predicts a good short-term prognosis and supports a purely mechanical (rather than purely inflammatory/structural) root irritation. If no centralisation occurs by week 3, or if leg symptoms/weakness worsen with any direction, flag to Dr. Satish for imaging review before progressing load.
Targets: L4–S1 segment mechanics the presumed common source of the back pain and the CPN pattern

5.2 Ankle-Foot Orthosis (AFO) fitting worn during all ambulation

Daily, all day
How: Fit a light, hinged or posterior-leaf-spring AFO bilaterally, holding the ankle at neutral (0° dorsiflexion) during gait.
Why: With CMAP amplitudes of only 0.63 mV (L) / 0.26 mV (R) versus a healthy >2 mV, voluntary dorsiflexion force is markedly reduced. An AFO mechanically substitutes for the lost force during swing phase, preventing foot-slap/trip/fall risk (§4), and prevents the ankle resting in plantarflexion between sessions directly countering §2.2 step 6 (Achilles contracture).
Interpretation: Success = no foot-slap or trips reported at week-6 review, and no loss of passive dorsiflexion ROM compared with baseline. Skin breakdown or a widening base of gait despite the AFO suggests the device needs re-fitting or that weakness has progressed escalate.
Targets: functional compensation for CPN motor deficit; Achilles length preservation

5.3 Neuromuscular Electrical Stimulation (NMES) to tibialis anterior / peroneus longus

15–20 min, 5×/week
How: Surface electrodes over the tibialis anterior motor point; pulsed biphasic current, 35–50 Hz, pulse width 250–350 µs, on:off ratio 1:3 to 1:2, intensity to visible/palpable contraction (not maximal tolerable pain), 10–15 contractions/session, ramping intensity weekly as tolerated.
Why (with the governing equation): The current needed to depolarise a motor axon to threshold follows the Weiss–Lapicque strength–duration relationship:
I(t) = I_rh · (1 + τ_c / t)
where I_rh is rheobase and τ_c is chronaxie. Larger, healthier motor axons have shorter chronaxie, so NMES preferentially recruits the motor units that remain most excitable exactly the population most likely to be re-innervating or spared. Repeated activation reduces disuse atrophy while the nerve itself is not yet contributing enough voluntary drive, and the paired sensory afferent volley re-enters the spinal cord, supporting central (spinal/cortical) re-mapping.
Interpretation: A rising palpable/visible contraction strength week-on-week at a stable stimulus intensity is a good early sign of motor unit recruitment. A contraction that stays flat or requires ever-higher intensity to achieve the same twitch by week 6 should be cross-checked against the week-12 interim NCS.
Targets: tibialis anterior / EDL motor units (CPN distribution)

5.4 Neurodynamic ("nerve gliding") mobilisation peroneal & sciatic tract

2 sets × 10 gentle oscillations, 4–5×/week
How: Slump-sit or supine straight-leg-raise base position; alternate ankle dorsiflexion/inversion with knee extension in a slow "sliding" (not tensioning) technique never into pain, never held static at end-range in Phase 1.
Why: Given the bilateral, root-level pattern (§1.3), the presumed site of irritation is proximal (near the L4–S1 exit or along the peroneal division of the sciatic trunk, see the pathway in §3). Gentle nerve sliding improves intraneural/perineural blood flow and reduces adhesions/mechanical tethering without adding tensile load to a nerve that is already electrically compromised supporting remyelination (§2.2 step 2) rather than provoking further injury.
Interpretation: Symptoms should not travel further down the leg during or after the glide. If tingling/pain spreads distally (peripheralises), the technique is too aggressive reduce range and revisit weekly.
Targets: mechanical interface of the L4–S1 roots / common peroneal nerve the pathway diagrammed in §3

5.5 Sustained gastrocnemius–soleus (Achilles) stretch

3 × 30–45 sec each side, daily; + night resting splint if tolerated
How: Standing wall calf stretch, knee straight (gastrocnemius) then knee bent (soleus); or seated towel-assisted stretch if standing balance is unsafe.
Why: The tibial nerve and its plantarflexors are electrically normal and therefore unopposed. Stretching maintains the muscle–tendon unit at functional length because the H-reflex data (§2.2 step 7) shows the tibial/S1 arc is intact, this protects healthy tissue from being shortened by a weak antagonist, not treating a nerve problem here.
Interpretation: Passive dorsiflexion ROM should stay within 5° of baseline throughout. Progressive loss of ROM despite compliant stretching signals the need for serial night splinting rather than more stretching alone.
Targets: gastrocnemius–soleus–Achilles complex (tibial nerve territory, intentionally spared)

5.6 Isometric & assisted-active dorsiflexion / toe extension

3 × 10 reps, 5×/week, both ankles
How: Seated, therapist or elastic band assists the ankle into dorsiflexion while the patient attempts to hold/initiate the movement actively; progress from fully-assisted to minimally-assisted as strength returns.
Why: This directly drives voluntary recruitment of the surviving CPN motor units, per Henneman's Size Principle (animated in §6) low-threshold units fire first at low effort, so early-phase work at low resistance is the correct way to begin re-education without fatiguing an already-reduced motor pool.
Interpretation: Any measurable active dorsiflexion excursion that was previously trace/absent is clinically meaningful, even if force is still low record it on the MMT grade scale at every session.
Targets: tibialis anterior, EDL, EDB voluntary drive

5.7 Deep core & lumbar segmental control (TrA / multifidus bracing)

3 × 10 × 6-sec holds, daily; progress to bird-dog by week 4
How: Hook-lying, teach a gentle abdominal-hollowing ("draw the navel in") co-contraction with the pelvic floor and transversus abdominis while breathing normally; progress to supported bird-dog (opposite arm/leg lift) once the brace can be held without back pain.
Why: Deep segmental muscles (TrA, multifidus) provide intersegmental stiffness that reduces shear load at the irritated L4–S1 segment during every subsequent leg exercise in this programme. Without this foundation, every later strengthening or gait drill risks re-loading the same root that is already compromised.
Interpretation: Ability to hold a 10-second brace with normal breathing and no back pain by week 4 is the minimum criterion to progress into Phase 2 loaded exercise.
Back–leg–foot link: This is the anchor exercise of the whole programme every leg-strengthening and gait drill from Phase 2 onward assumes this brace is available as a background stabiliser, which is precisely how §2.2 step 8's compensation loop gets broken rather than perpetuated.
Targets: lumbar segmental stability, protecting the L4–S1 root during all leg work

PHASE 2 Weeks 7–12: Progressive strengthening, lumbar stabilisation, and load re-introduction

Goal: convert activated motor units into functional strength, progress core stability into dynamic control, and begin light nerve tensioning once symptoms have settled.

5.8 Progressive resisted dorsiflexion (elastic band / ankle weight)

3 × 12–15 reps, 4×/week; progress load every 2 weeks if MMT improves ≥ 1 grade
How: Seated or long-sitting, resistance band looped over forefoot anchored distally; pull ankle into dorsiflexion against resistance through full available range, controlled 2-sec concentric / 3-sec eccentric tempo.
Why: As motor unit pool size (CMAP amplitude) increases through reinnervation/hypertrophy, higher loads now recruit progressively larger, higher-threshold units (Size Principle again, see the recruitment animation below) this is how strength training converts "activation" into functional torque.
Henneman's Size Principle: small, fatigue-resistant motor units (left) fire first at low effort; larger, higher-force units (right) join in only as load/effort rises this is why Phase 1 used low resistance and Phase 2 progressively increases it.
Interpretation: An MMT grade improvement of ≥1 by week 12 in either dorsiflexor group is the pre-agreed threshold to continue progressive loading unchanged. Failure to reach this threshold is an escalation trigger (§7).
Targets: tibialis anterior / EDL force generation

5.9 Functional Electrical Stimulation (FES) during treadmill/overground gait

10–15 min/session, 3×/week
How: Foot-switch or tilt-sensor-triggered FES unit stimulates dorsiflexors precisely during the swing phase of gait, replacing the static AFO for supervised sessions once tolerated.
Why: Timed, task-specific stimulation reinforces the correct temporal pattern of muscle activation for gait a form of motor learning/re-patterning a static brace cannot provide while exercising the exact functionally deficient movement (swing-phase dorsiflexion).
Interpretation: Progressive reduction in the FES trigger threshold needed to produce a visible step improvement suggests increasing voluntary contribution; if FES-assisted gait shows no change in step symmetry by week 12, review AFO fit and re-screen MMT before continuing unchanged.
Targets: temporal motor control, CPN-innervated dorsiflexors

5.10 Single-leg balance & proprioceptive training

3 × 30–60 sec holds per leg, progressing surface difficulty, 4×/week
How: Progress: double-leg stance eyes open → eyes closed → single-leg stance → foam/wobble board, always with stable support nearby.
Why: With CPN motor units reduced, ankle proprioceptive/stabilising strategy is compromised distally; balance training drives central compensation (hip strategy, visual/vestibular input) to reduce fall risk while distal recovery is still in progress.
Interpretation: Increasing single-leg stance time on a firm surface by ≥50% from week 7 to week 12 indicates good central compensation is being built; no progress suggests balance work should be supervised more closely and surface difficulty regressed.
Targets: fall-risk reduction, central postural compensation

5.11 Continue neurodynamic glides progress to gentle tensioning

2–3 sets × 10, 4×/week
How: Once symptoms/irritability have settled (no worsening of paraesthesia or pain from Phase 1), introduce light tensioning (holding brief end-range positions for 2–3 sec) rather than pure sliding, following standard neurodynamic progression principles.
Why: Light tensioning further improves neural mobility at the presumed root/nerve interface once the tissue is less irritable, building on the sliding work of Phase 1.
Interpretation: No increase in back or leg symptoms 24 hours after introducing tension confirms readiness; any flare means reverting to sliding-only technique for another 2 weeks before re-attempting.
Targets: L4–S1 root / CPN mechanical interface

5.12 Dynamic lumbar-pelvic stabilisation progression

3 × 10–12 reps each drill, 4×/week
How: Progress from Phase-1 bird-dog into glute bridges (double- then single-leg), side-plank holds (regressed to knees if needed), and dead-bug pattern, all performed with the brace from 5.7 maintained throughout.
Why: Bridging and side-plank work specifically recruit gluteus medius/maximus and the lateral trunk, which share load with the lumbar spine during single-leg stance and gait directly reducing the compensatory hip-hike/circumduction pattern identified in §4 that would otherwise keep overloading the L4–S1 segment.
Interpretation: Ability to hold a single-leg bridge for 20 seconds without pelvic drop or back pain by week 12 indicates the hip/trunk is now sharing load appropriately; persistent pelvic drop (a positive Trendelenburg pattern) should be specifically re-targeted before progressing to Phase 3 gait retraining.
Back–leg–foot link: This is the direct bridge (literally and functionally) between the core work of Phase 1 and the gait retraining of Phase 3 it teaches the hip and trunk to compensate for ankle weakness in a controlled, low lumbar-load way, rather than the spine absorbing that compensation by default.
Targets: gluteal/lateral trunk load-sharing, lumbar protection during gait

5.13 Week-12 interim re-assessment

End of Phase 2

Repeat: MMT (tibialis anterior, EDL, EDB, peroneals), ankle ROM, lumbar ROM/pain pattern, functional gait assessment, and request an interim CPN motor NCS from the referring neurologist.

Interpretation: Given the regeneration math in §2.4, week 12 is too early to expect the CPN CMAP amplitude to have normalised, but a small, real rise (e.g. left CPN ankle amplitude moving from 0.63 mV toward 0.9–1.2 mV) is a meaningful positive signal that reinnervation is underway and the programme should continue unchanged into Phase 3. A flat or falling amplitude at week 12 is the trigger to loop back to Dr. Satish/Dr. Santosh for consideration of lumbosacral MRI before continuing.

PHASE 3 Weeks 13–18: Functional integration, gait retraining with lumbo-pelvic control

Goal: re-integrate strength and stability gains into complex, real-world gait and functional tasks, while continuing to protect the lumbar segment.

5.14 Task-specific gait retraining (uneven surfaces, stairs, dual-task)

15–20 min, 3×/week
How: Progress from level indoor walking to uneven outdoor surfaces, stair ascent/descent with rail support, and dual-task walking (e.g. carrying an object, counting backwards) to stress-test automaticity.
Why: Strength and isolated motor control (Phases 1–2) must be re-integrated into complex, real-world gait demands to transfer clinic gains into safe community ambulation, and to confirm the lumbo-pelvic control built in 5.12 holds up under distraction and terrain challenge.
Interpretation: Maintained step symmetry and no reported back pain flare during dual-task walking by week 18 indicates the compensation strategy is now automatic and protective, not effortful and provocative.
Targets: functional carry-over, fall-risk in daily life

5.15 Functional lifting mechanics & hip-hinge training

3 × 8–10 reps, 3×/week, bodyweight then light load
How: Teach the hip-hinge pattern (bending at the hips with a neutral spine, not the lumbar spine) for all functional bending/lifting tasks the patient performs daily (e.g. picking up items, gardening, kitchen work); progress from bodyweight to a light kettlebell/basket once the pattern is consistent.
Why: Daily bending is a major, repeated source of lumbar loading. Converting the movement strategy from a lumbar-flexion-dominant pattern to a hip-dominant pattern removes a large, avoidable contributor to ongoing root irritation directly addressing the back-pain component of the presenting complaint, not just the leg.
Interpretation: Patient demonstrating the hinge pattern automatically (without cueing) during a simulated daily task by week 18 is the target; persistent lumbar-flexion-dominant lifting despite cueing should prompt a home/workplace ergonomic review.
Targets: reducing repetitive lumbar flexion load in daily activity

5.16 Advanced neurodynamic & combined-movement testing

2 sets × 10, 3×/week
How: Progress neurodynamic sequences to combined movements (e.g. slump + cervical flexion + ankle dorsiflexion) if tolerated, matching the patient's functional postures (sitting to standing, stair climbing).
Why: By this stage, tissue irritability should be low enough to test the nerve's mobility in the actual postures that provoke symptoms in daily life, giving a more functionally relevant readout than isolated straight-leg-raise testing alone.
Interpretation: A negative (symptom-free) combined slump test by week 18 is a good prognostic sign for continued root recovery; a still-positive test at this stage should be reported at the week-24 review alongside the repeat NCS.
Targets: functional neural mobility across the L4–S1/CPN pathway

PHASE 4 Weeks 19–24: Endurance, full functional return, and final objective re-assessment

Goal: build fatigue resistance in the newly-recruited motor pool, confirm safe independence from compensatory devices where earned, and objectively re-measure nerve status against the original baseline.

5.17 Endurance dosing repeated sit-to-stand, stair ascent/descent, treadmill

3 × 10–15 reps or 10–15 min continuous, 3×/week
How: Progress duration/repetitions weekly as tolerated; monitor for compensatory pattern breakdown (foot-slap returning, hip-hike returning, back pain onset) as the fatigue signal to stop that session's set.
Why: Motor unit re-innervation increases the number of available units, but each may still fatigue faster than normal; endurance dosing specifically trains fatigue resistance of the newly-recruited pool rather than just peak force.
Interpretation: Ability to sustain 15 minutes of continuous gait without compensatory pattern breakdown by week 24 is the functional endurance target.
Targets: fatigue resistance of CPN-innervated muscles

5.18 Wean AFO for indoor/level surfaces only if MMT ≥ 4/5 and no foot-slap observed

Reassess weekly
Why: Bracing should be removed only when voluntary dorsiflexor force is objectively sufficient to clear the foot in swing without compensatory hip-hiking or circumduction removing it earlier risks falls and a return of the lumbar-loading compensation loop (§2.2 step 8); keeping it indefinitely once strength returns risks unnecessary disuse.
Interpretation: A successful indoor wean with no gait deviation on video analysis is the criterion to progress to outdoor/uneven-surface weaning; any deviation means continuing the AFO for those surfaces regardless of MMT grade.
Targets: safe transition from compensation to true recovery

5.19 Advanced balance, agility & return-to-activity retraining

15–20 min, 3×/week
How: Introduce direction changes, step-overs, uneven terrain walks, and any specific activities the patient wants to return to (household, social, occupational), matched to her actual daily demands.
Why: Return-to-function is the true endpoint of rehabilitation clinic-based strength and balance gains must be proven under the specific demands the patient will face after discharge from supervised sessions.
Interpretation: Independent, confident performance of the patient's own priority activities by week 24 is the functional discharge criterion, alongside the objective NCS comparison below.
Targets: patient-specific functional goals

5.20 Week-24 final re-assessment

End of 6-month programme

Repeat: MMT (tibialis anterior, EDL, EDB, peroneals), ankle ROM, lumbar ROM/pain pattern, combined neurodynamic testing, functional gait assessment, and request a repeat CPN motor NCS and H-reflex from the referring neurologist for objective comparison against the week-0 baselines in §1.1 and the week-12 interim study.

Interpretation: Given the §2.4 regeneration math (≈8–25 months for full-distance reinnervation from a root-level lesion), a realistic "good outcome" at 6 months is: (a) clear, measurable CMAP amplitude improvement even if not yet normalised, (b) MMT improved by ≥1–2 grades in both dorsiflexor groups, (c) resolved or substantially improved back pain with a negative/near-negative neurodynamic test, and (d) safe, AFO-free indoor ambulation. Meeting most but not all of these criteria supports moving into the extended maintenance phase (§8) rather than either premature discharge or escalation. Meeting none of them, or any objective worsening on NCS, is the trigger for lumbosacral spine imaging and neurosurgical/spine-specialist referral.

6Precautions & escalation criteria

  • Red flags stop and refer back immediately if new/worsening saddle numbness, bladder/bowel symptoms, or rapidly progressive bilateral leg weakness appear at any point (cauda equina screen) this must be actively screened at every visit given the root-level, bilateral pattern and the co-existing back pain.
  • Do not push neurodynamic techniques into reproduction of radicular pain, tingling, or numbness glide, don't tension, until irritability settles (Phase 1 rule; re-apply if any flare recurs in later phases).
  • Do not progress lumbar loading (5.12, 5.15) if directional-preference testing (5.1) has not yet identified a clear centralising direction.
  • Monitor skin under the AFO weekly for pressure areas.
  • If MMT does not improve by at least one grade in dorsiflexors by week 12, flag to the referring neurologist this may indicate the axonal component is more severe than the amplitude data alone suggested, or that a structural (disc/root compression) cause needs imaging.
  • Falls-risk precautions (supervised gait, appropriate footwear, clear walking surfaces) apply for the entire 24 weeks given bilateral involvement.
  • Any objective worsening (falling CMAP amplitude, slowing CV, or new denervation potentials) on the week-12 or week-24 repeat NCS overrides the phase-based progression above pause loading progression and refer back immediately.

7If 6 months is not enough extending into a 12-month maintenance/advanced phase

Because a root-level lesion can plausibly take up to ~25 months to fully reinnervate the distal foot muscles (§2.4), it is entirely realistic that week-24 re-testing shows real but incomplete recovery. Rather than treating 24 weeks as a hard stop, this document recommends the following extension logic:

Months 7–9 (Weeks 25–36): Continue Phase-4-level strengthening/endurance/gait work at a reduced supervised frequency (1–2×/week) with a fuller home programme; repeat NCS at month 9 if amplitude was still rising at month 6.
Months 10–12 (Weeks 37–52): Maintenance dosing (2–3×/week home programme, monthly clinical review), progressing toward full AFO independence and unrestricted activity if trends remain positive; final NCS and MMT comparison at month 12 to close the loop against the original §1.1 baseline.

This extension pathway is offered as a planning framework; the treating physiotherapist and referring physicians should confirm the need for it based on the actual week-12 and week-24 findings rather than adopting it by default.
