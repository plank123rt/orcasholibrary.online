01 · Stomach

Gastric acid chemistry

Parietal cells in the stomach lining pump protons into the lumen, turning the stomach into a pH ≈ 1.5–3.5 environment. That acidity unfolds dietary protein so digestive enzymes can reach the peptide bonds inside — the first link in a chain that ends at the kidney.

Reaction chain
carbonic anhydrase
CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3−
H⁺/K⁺ ATPase, into gastric lumen
H+ (cell) → H+ (lumen)
H+ + Cl− → HCl
Every H⁺ pumped out leaves an HCO₃⁻ behind in the blood — the same bicarbonate that reappears in step 05's acid–base equation, on its way to being rebalanced by the kidney.

02 · Small intestine

Protein hydrolysis

Once unfolded, proteins are cut into progressively smaller pieces by a relay of enzymes — each specific to a different bond, each governed by the same saturating kinetics.

Enzyme relay
Protein + H2O --Pepsin--> Peptides
Peptides + H2O --Trypsin / Chymotrypsin--> Oligopeptides
Oligopeptides --Peptidases--> Amino acids
Cleavage in motion

03 · Intestinal wall

Nutrient absorption

Amino acids, glucose and fats each cross into the bloodstream by a different route — but glucose and amino acids share a trick: they hitch a ride on the sodium gradient built by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump.

Villus cross-section
Why surface area matters
~30×
surface increase from folds + villi + microvilli
Na⁺
gradient powers glucose & amino acid entry
Glucose and amino acids move against their own gradient by co-transport with Na⁺, which is itself kept low inside the cell by the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase — the same pump that reappears in step 04, spending the ATP that bioenergetics produces.
04 · Mitochondria

Cellular bioenergetics

Absorbed glucose is oxidized in three linked stages. Each hands electrons to the next, ending with ATP synthesis at the electron transport chain — the shared energy currency for every active process downstream, including the kidney's own pumps.

Oxidation pipeline
C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + ~30–32 ATP
NAD+ + 2e− + H+ ⇌ NADH
Where the ATP goes
0 ATP / glucose
That ATP is spent almost as fast as it's made — largely on the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase (step 03) and the same pump family that maintains pressure and transport at the kidney in step 06.
05 · Blood & extracellular fluid

Acid–base & electrolyte balance

Blood pH is held in an unusually narrow band by the ratio of bicarbonate to dissolved CO₂ — a ratio the lungs and kidneys constantly re-adjust, using the same ions absorbed in step 03.

Henderson–Hasselbalch
pH = pKa + log( [HCO3−] / (0.03 × PCO2) )

06 · Kidney — where every prior stage converges

Renal filtration & clearance

Digestion supplied the raw materials, bioenergetics supplied the ATP, and acid–base balance supplied stable electrolytes — all of it in service of this one physical checkpoint. The glomerulus filters blood under three opposing pressures, and what crosses depends on that pressure balance and the filter's pore size, not on any enzyme.

Starling forces at the glomerulus
Net Filtration Pressure = PGC − PBS − πGC
GFR ≈ Kf × Net Filtration Pressure
Net filtration pressure (auto-cycling)10 mmHg
The filtration membrane — size decides what crosses
Creatine phosphate → Creatinine → filtered → excreted
Creatinine forms at a steady, largely non-enzymatic rate from muscle creatine and leaves the body almost entirely by crossing this filter — so its blood level tracks filtration rate, exactly like the pressure relationship in the chart beside it.
Same filter, a second molecule: uric acid
Purines → Hypoxanthine → Xanthine → Uric Acid (xanthine oxidase)
Uric acid is produced by a separate enzyme chain but cleared the same way — through the glomerulus. Its serum level, like creatinine's, reflects production versus this shared clearance route.
