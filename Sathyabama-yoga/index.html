Sciatic pathway, simplified

One pathway, two checkpoints.

Signals for lifting the foot leave the spinal cord at the S1 nerve root, travel down the sciatic nerve, then split at the knee. One branch wraps around the outer knee (the fibular neck) as the common peroneal nerve, on its way to the foot-lifting muscles. Your study found gentle slowing at both of these checkpoints, on both legs. Nothing broken, simply signals moving a little slower than usual.

Why this approach

Why yoga, and why this particular sequence

Every pose below has one job: to either wake up the exact muscles the peroneal nerve drives, glide the nerve gently through the very checkpoints your study identified, or free up the S1 nerve root at the spine. Nothing here is generic. Each posture is chosen for the pathway shown on your report.

Wake the foot-lifters

Slow, repeated ankle and toe movements re-activate the tibialis anterior (the muscle the peroneal nerve controls), helping strengthen exactly what showed reduced amplitude on your test.

Glide, don't stretch, the nerve

Nerves heal best with gentle, rhythmic movement through their length, not forceful stretching. Poses that combine a slow knee-bend with an ankle flex do exactly this along the sciatic-to-peroneal line.

Free the S1 root

Spinal mobility and gentle low-back strengthening keep the S1 nerve root (the origin point of the delayed H-reflex) unrestricted as it exits the spine.

Positions to set aside for now

  • Long kneeling or sitting on the heels (Vajrasana held for many minutes). This presses directly on the outer knee, exactly where your peroneal nerve is already running slow.
  • Sitting with legs crossed at the knee for long stretches, same pressure point, same nerve.
  • Deep forward bends with the knees locked straight , keep a soft bend in the knee so the sciatic nerve is glided, never yanked.

What helps every session

  • A folded blanket under the outer knee whenever you sit on the floor, to keep pressure off the fibular neck.
  • Every stretch paired with a slow, unhurried breath. The nerve settles faster when the body isn't guarding.
  • Daily, gentle repetition beats occasional intensity. Nerves respond to consistent, small signals over weeks.
  • Change position if you've been sitting still for more than 20–30 minutes.
Your sequence · 25 minutes

The practice, one posture at a time

Move through these in order. Keep every movement inside a comfortable range. Mild sensation is fine, sharp or shooting pain is the signal to ease back.

Slow Abdominal Breathing

Dirga Svasam
Position: seated or lying downDuration: 2 minutes
  1. Sit tall or lie on your back with knees bent, feet flat.
  2. Rest both hands on your belly. Inhale for a count of 4, feeling the belly rise.
  3. Exhale slowly for a count of 6, letting the belly fall completely.
Why for you: A settled nervous system lets the legs move without guarding or tightening, which is exactly the state the rest of the sequence needs.

Ankle Circles & Toe Taps

Adapted Chakki Chalanasana for the ankle
Position: seated, legs extendedDuration: 10 circles each way, 15 taps, both feet
  1. Sit with legs extended, hands slightly behind you for support.
  2. Circle each ankle slowly, 10 times in each direction.
  3. Then lift the toes and front of the foot up toward you, tap down, and repeat 15 times per foot.
Why for you: This is a direct, active workout for the tibialis anterior, the exact muscle your slowed, lower-amplitude peroneal nerve supplies. Repetition here is the most targeted thing in the whole sequence.

Cat – Cow

Marjaryasana – Bitilasana
Position: hands and knees, on a folded blanketDuration: 8–10 slow rounds
  1. Come onto hands and knees, a folded blanket under the knees.
  2. Inhale, drop the belly, lift the chest and tailbone (Cow).
  3. Exhale, round the spine, tuck the chin and tailbone (Cat).
  4. Move slowly, one breath per movement.
Why for you: Gentle, rhythmic movement through the lower spine keeps the S1 nerve root (the origin of your delayed H-reflex) moving freely as it exits the spinal column.

Reclining Hand-to-Big-Toe (with strap)

Supta Padangusthasana
Position: lying on backDuration: 6 slow reps, both legs
  1. Lie on your back, loop a scarf or belt around the ball of one foot.
  2. Straighten that leg toward the ceiling only as far as is comfortable, a soft bend in the knee is fine.
  3. Slowly flex the ankle (toes toward you), then point it, 6 times, keeping the leg still.
  4. Lower and switch sides.
Why for you: The ankle-pump-while-leg-is-raised motion gently glides the sciatic and tibial nerve, the same pathway your H-reflex travels, without ever forcing a hard stretch.

Bridge Pose

Setu Bandhasana
Position: lying on back, knees bentDuration: hold 5 breaths × 4 rounds
  1. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet hip-width apart flat on the floor.
  2. Press feet down, lift the hips gently toward the ceiling.
  3. Hold for 5 slow breaths, then lower with control.
Why for you: This engages the gluteal muscles at the S1 level (the same segment your H-reflex tests) and gently extends the lower spine, easing pressure at the nerve root.

Modified Cobra

Ardha Bhujangasana
Position: lying on stomachDuration: hold 5 breaths × 4 rounds
  1. Lie on your stomach, forearms on the floor, elbows under shoulders.
  2. Inhale and lift the chest just slightly, keeping the low back long, not crunched.
  3. Hold 5 breaths, then rest with the head turned to one side.
Why for you: A small, controlled lift strengthens the low-back muscles that support the S1 nerve root, without the strain a deeper backbend would place on it.

Standing Heel-Toe Rock

Tadasana variation
Position: standing, near a wall for balanceDuration: 12 slow rocks
  1. Stand tall, one hand lightly on a wall or chair for balance.
  2. Slowly rock forward onto the toes, then back onto the heels, lifting the front of the foot as high as feels controlled.
  3. Move slowly, this is about control, not speed.
Why for you: This retrains the foot-lifting motion in a standing, functional position, carrying the strength you're building straight into your everyday walking pattern.

Legs Up the Wall

Viparita Karani
Position: lying near a wallDuration: 5–8 minutes
  1. Sit sideways against a wall, then swing your legs up as you lie back.
  2. Let the legs rest fully supported by the wall, arms relaxed by your sides.
  3. Breathe slowly and stay as long as is comfortable.
Why for you: A fully supported, passive position lets both nerve pathways rest completely after being gently worked, the quiet counterpart to the active poses above.

Closing Rest

Shavasana
Position: lying flat, fully supportedDuration: 3–5 minutes
  1. Lie flat, legs relaxed and slightly apart, arms by your sides, palms up.
  2. Let the breath return to its natural rhythm.
  3. Before rising, gently wiggle the toes and ankles, then roll to one side.
Why for you: Closing in stillness lets everything you've just activated settle and integrate, rather than moving straight from effort into the rest of your day.
Building the habit

A rhythm for the week

Nerves respond to steady, repeated signals rather than occasional long sessions. A short morning practice plus one full evening session is more effective than one long weekly effort.

Morning · 10 minutes

Wake the pathway

  1. Slow Abdominal Breathing (1 minute)
  2. Ankle Circles & Toe Taps (full set)
  3. Cat – Cow (8 rounds)
  4. Standing Heel-Toe Rock (12 rocks)
Evening · 25 minutes

The full sequence

  1. All nine postures, in the order given
  2. Move slowly, there is no rush between poses
  3. Close every session in Shavasana, even on short days
