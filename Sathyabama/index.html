Why a nerve signal changes like this

A short explainer on what's actually happening inside the nerve, in plain language first, then in the clinical terms your report uses.

Think of a motor nerve as a thick electrical cable made up of many individual wires bundled together, each one called an axon. Each axon carries the same instruction ("lift the foot") from the spine down to the muscle. A healthy nerve fires all, or nearly all, of its wires together, so the message arrives strong and on time. When your report says the peroneal nerve shows an axonal pattern with reduced amplitude and mild slowing, it means a portion of those individual wires are no longer conducting properly, so fewer signals reach the muscle (lower amplitude) and the overall average speed drops slightly (lower conduction velocity), even though the fastest remaining wires still start on time (which is why your onset latency, the DML, stayed normal).

Conduction velocity = distance travelled ÷ time taken  —  a healthy peroneal nerve typically runs 40–50+ m/s

What could actually be doing this? Possible reasons

The written report doesn't name a single cause, it flags the pattern and asks for clinical correlation. Here are the usual suspects behind a bilateral peroneal axonal pattern like this one, in plain terms and then in the scientific reasoning. This is background information, not a diagnosis of what's happening in this case.

1

A pinch point near the knee (mechanical)

The peroneal nerve wraps around a bony bump just below the knee (the fibular head) with almost no cushioning over it. It's the single most "pinch-prone" nerve in the leg. Crossing the legs for long periods, squatting, sitting a certain way, tight bandages, or even recent weight loss reducing the natural padding there can all press on it.

Why it fits: focal compression damages the axons right at the pressure point, which shows up as reduced amplitude and mild slowing below that point, matching what shows up on both sides here.

2

Reduced blood flow to the nerve (vascular)

Nerves are living tissue and need their own tiny blood vessels to stay fed. This is not about the blood itself being "thick," it's about whether enough blood is reaching the nerve fibers moment to moment. If those small vessels narrow, from long-standing high blood sugar, cholesterol buildup, or occasionally inflammation of small vessels, the nerve fibers gradually lose function.

Why it fits: the larger, faster-conducting axons tend to be the most sensitive to reduced blood supply, and losing them first produces exactly this mix, lower amplitude with only mild velocity slowing, rather than a severe uniform slowing.

3

A body-wide metabolic factor

Conditions such as diabetes, thyroid imbalance, vitamin B12 deficiency, or long-term alcohol use can make nerves more fragile everywhere, so they get injured more easily from ordinary pressure that a healthy nerve would tolerate without any trouble.

Why it fits: these processes typically affect the longest, most exposed nerves first, and the peroneal nerve, being long and running very close to the skin at the knee, is one of the earliest to show changes even when a metabolic cause is still mild.

4

A nerve-root contribution (the "radiculo" in the report)

The word "radiculo" in the impression means the doctor also wants to rule out whether the nerve root as it exits the lower spine (commonly the L5 level) is contributing, since pressure there can send weakness down a very similar path.

Why it fits: telling a pure peroneal nerve problem apart from an L5 nerve-root problem usually needs a hands-on exam of muscles that share the L5 root but are supplied by a different nerve, or imaging of the lower spine if there's any doubt.

How the back pain and the leg finding could connect

Cause #4 above, the nerve-root contribution, is worth tracing out visually, because it's the one explanation that ties the back pain and the leg findings to a single spot in the spine.

L4, L5, and S1 nerve roots become the sciatic nerve, which splits into the tibial and peroneal nerves.

The L4, L5, and S1 roots shown above are the same roots that later become the peroneal and tibial nerves in the leg. One plausible chain of events: these roots exit the spine at the lower back through small bony openings; if something at that level, a bulging disc, canal narrowing, or joint swelling, presses on or irritates a root, it can do two things at once, irritate local pain fibers (felt as back pain) and slowly affect the motor fibers travelling down that root (showing up downstream as the reduced peroneal signal). That would also fit why the arm nerves, served by a different part of the spine, tested completely normal.

What this could mean over time

Axonal nerve findings like this one don't have a single fixed outcome. What happens next depends heavily on the underlying cause and whether it's addressed. Here are three general directions a finding like this can take,

If the cause is positional or mechanical

A pinched nerve that's caught early and relieved (changing how you sit, sleep, or cross your legs) often recovers substantially over weeks to months, since injured axons can slowly regrow, roughly at the pace of about 1 millimeter a day.

If it's mild and the cause isn't fully clear yet

The usual next step is watchful monitoring: a repeat nerve study in a few months to see if the numbers are holding steady, plus keeping an eye out for any new tripping, fatigue with walking, or a "foot slap" when walking.

If an underlying driver goes unmanaged

If something like uncontrolled blood sugar or ongoing compression continues, axon loss can gradually increase, raising the risk of a fuller foot drop, an unsteady gait, and a higher chance of trips or falls, sometimes eventually needing a light ankle-foot brace to keep the foot up while walking.

In plain terms

What this points to

"Suggestive of bilateral CPN motor axonal radiculo neuropathy."

The peroneal nerve runs down the outside of the knee and shin, and it's the one that lifts your foot and toes. On both legs, it's showing a smaller, slower signal than expected, a pattern that usually points to some nerve fibers not conducting as well as they should (an "axonal" pattern) rather than a problem with the wrist, hand, or arm nerves, which all tested normal. The reflex loop through the spinal cord is also a little slow on both sides, which is being looked at alongside the nerve finding. As the sections above walk through, this pattern can come from local pressure on the nerve near the knee, reduced blood flow to the nerve, a body-wide metabolic factor, or a nerve-root contribution.
