Celiac disease — the complete mechanism

Not "gluten is bad." A specific chain of molecular events: a protein fragment that resists digestion, an enzyme that chemically alters it, a genetically inherited receptor that displays it to the immune system, and a two-pronged immune response — one arm producing antibodies, the other directly killing gut cells — that together flatten the structures the small intestine uses to absorb food. Every step below, in order, with the biology behind each one.

This is mechanism, not a diagnosis — that requires the blood tests and biopsy described near the end. Sources are listed at the bottom.

The villi are the finger-like projections that give the small intestine its absorptive surface area. When they flatten (villous atrophy), that surface area collapses, and food passes through largely unabsorbed — the end result of everything below.

01 — Definition

What celiac disease actually is

Celiac disease is a chronic autoimmune disorder in which dietary gluten — a protein family in wheat, barley, and rye — triggers the immune system to attack the lining of the small intestine, in people who carry a specific genetic susceptibility.[1] It affects roughly 1 in 100 people worldwide, though most cases go undiagnosed for years because the symptoms are so variable.[4]

Two things have to be true at once for it to happen: the genetic susceptibility (a particular immune-receptor gene) has to be present, and gluten has to be eaten. Neither alone is sufficient — carrying the gene without eating gluten never triggers disease, and eating gluten without the gene doesn't either. That's why it runs in families but doesn't affect every relative equally.

02 — The trigger

Why gluten survives digestion intact

Gliadin, the reactive component of gluten, is unusually rich in the amino acids proline and glutamine. Human digestive enzymes are poor at cutting peptide bonds next to proline, so instead of being broken all the way down into single amino acids like most protein, gliadin leaves behind larger fragments — some over 30 amino acids long — that survive intact into the small intestine.[8] One of these fragments, a 33-amino-acid piece of alpha-gliadin, turns out to be exactly the piece the immune system reacts to.

These leftover fragments then have to get from the gut lumen (where digestion happens) into the tissue underneath (the lamina propria, where immune cells live) to cause any trouble. They cross by two routes: a receptor called CD71, normally used to transport iron-carrying proteins, can be hijacked to ferry gliadin-antibody complexes across the epithelial cells; fragments can also slip between cells if the tight junctions holding them together loosen.[4]

03 — The chemical modification

Tissue transglutaminase rewrites the peptide

Once in the lamina propria, the gliadin fragment meets an enzyme called tissue transglutaminase 2 (TG2 or tTG) — an enzyme the body normally uses for wound healing and tissue repair, released into the area during any local inflammation.[3] TG2 performs a specific chemical reaction called deamidation: it converts certain glutamine residues in the gliadin peptide into glutamate, which carries a negative electrical charge that glutamine doesn't have.[8]

That single change matters enormously, because the receptor in the next step — the one that decides whether the immune system ever sees this peptide — binds negatively charged residues far more tightly than neutral ones. Deamidation isn't incidental damage; it's the step that converts a harmless food fragment into a peptide the immune system can actually latch onto.[8]

TG2's second job

TG2 is also the target of the antibodies celiac blood tests measure (anti-tTG). The body ends up making antibodies against its own repair enzyme, not just against gluten.[3]

Not a food allergy

This is a T-cell-driven autoimmune reaction, mechanistically distinct from an IgE food allergy — which is why it doesn't cause hives or anaphylaxis, but does cause lasting tissue damage.[1]

04 — The genetic gatekeeper

HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8: why genetics decide who's even eligible

The modified peptide still has to be shown to the immune system, and that job belongs to a specific receptor sitting on antigen-presenting cells: HLA-DQ2 (found in over 90% of people with celiac disease) or, in most of the remainder, HLA-DQ8.[4] These are inherited immune-recognition molecules — the same general family used to distinguish "self" from "foreign" — and their binding groove happens to have a strong preference for negatively charged residues, exactly what deamidation produces.[8]

About 30–40% of the general population carries HLA-DQ2 or DQ8, but only roughly 1% develops celiac disease — so the gene is necessary but nowhere near sufficient.[4] This is also why genetic testing is used to rule out celiac disease (a negative result makes it very unlikely) rather than to confirm it (a positive result just means the door is unlocked, not that anyone walked through it).

The full molecular cascade, step by step

Each box lights up in sequence — this is the order the biology actually happens in.

Two immune arms run in parallel from the same trigger: the adaptive arm (T cells → cytokines → B cells → antibodies) and the innate arm (IL-15 directly arming intraepithelial lymphocytes as killer cells). Both converge on the same outcome — a flattened, inflamed gut lining.[2]

05 & 06 — The two immune arms

Antibodies on one side, direct killing on the other

The adaptive arm. The activated CD4+ T cell polarizes into a Th1-dominant response, releasing interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and IL-21. These cytokines do two things: they recruit and activate cytotoxic immune cells in the gut lining, and they help B cells mature into antibody-producing plasma cells.[2] The antibodies produced — against tissue transglutaminase, against endomysium (connective tissue), and against the deamidated gliadin peptide itself — are what standard celiac blood tests actually measure. They're a marker of the immune response, not directly the cause of the tissue damage.

The innate arm. Separately, a different gliadin fragment (called p31–43) isn't recognized by T cells at all, but directly triggers gut epithelial cells and dendritic cells to release IL-15.[2],[8] IL-15 arms intraepithelial lymphocytes — immune cells that already live embedded in the gut lining — turning them into cytotoxic killers that directly destroy the epithelial cells around them, independent of the antibody response. This innate pathway helps explain why gut damage can be extensive even when antibody levels don't perfectly track symptom severity.

07 — What the biopsy actually shows

The Marsh classification: grading the damage

This is the histological scale a pathologist uses on a small-bowel biopsy sample.[5],[6]

0

Normal

No increase in intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs), normal villous architecture. Doesn't rule out celiac disease if serology is positive — early or patchy disease can look normal on a given sample.

1

Infiltrative

Raised IEL count (typically over 25–30 per 100 enterocytes), but villi and crypts still look structurally normal. Suggestive but not confirmatory on its own.

2

Hyperplastic

Raised IELs plus crypt hyperplasia — the crypts elongate as the tissue tries to regenerate faster than it's being damaged. Villi are still intact at this stage.

3a–3c

Villous atrophy (diagnostic)

Partial (3a), subtotal (3b), or total (3c) flattening of the villi, on top of crypt hyperplasia and raised IELs. Marsh 3 with positive serology is what confirms the diagnosis.[6]

08 — The downstream effect

Malabsorption, nutrient by nutrient

Where in the gut a nutrient is normally absorbed determines how early it's affected — celiac damage is worst in the duodenum and upper jejunum, and tapers off further down.[6]

Iron & folate

Absorbed almost entirely in the duodenum — exactly where villous damage is worst — so deficiency shows up early and is often the first clue, sometimes before any GI symptoms appear.[6]

Vitamin B12

Absorbed further downstream in the terminal ileum, which is usually spared even in fairly severe disease — so B12 often stays normal even when iron and folate are already low.[6]

Calcium & vitamin D

Reduced absorption over time leads to low bone density and, in children, can slow growth; in adults it raises long-term osteoporosis risk.[5]

Fat & fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)

Damaged villi and reduced enzyme output impair fat absorption generally, which drags these vitamins down with it.

Protein & calories overall

The cumulative effect of reduced absorptive surface area — this is the direct mechanistic link to unexplained weight loss despite normal intake.

09 — Why it's so often missed

It doesn't have to look like a stomach problem

Classic presentation

Diarrhea, bloating, abdominal pain, weight loss — the textbook picture, but only a minority of cases present this way.

Silent / atypical presentation

Iron-deficiency anemia, unexplained fatigue, osteoporosis, elevated liver enzymes, infertility, or — in teenagers — slowed growth or delayed puberty, with normal-looking digestion.[5]

Dermatitis herpetiformis

An intensely itchy blistering skin rash, driven by the same anti-tTG immune response depositing in the skin — considered the cutaneous form of celiac disease.

Neurological signs

Peripheral neuropathy, chronic headache, and balance problems have been linked to untreated celiac disease in some patients, though the mechanism is less well defined than the gut findings.

10 — Confirming it

How it's actually diagnosed

Step one: serology, while still eating gluten. Blood tests measure the antibodies produced by the adaptive immune response described above: anti-tTG IgA is the standard first test, alongside a total IgA level (about 2–3% of people with celiac disease are IgA-deficient, which would make anti-tTG IgA falsely negative — in that case, IgG-based versions of the same tests are used instead).[5],[6]

Step two: duodenal biopsy. Multiple small samples are taken during upper endoscopy and graded on the Marsh scale above. This remains the diagnostic gold standard in most guidelines, though some recent pediatric guidelines allow skipping biopsy when antibody titers are extremely high (commonly defined as more than 10 times the upper limit of normal) alongside a second positive antibody and compatible symptoms.[6]

Genetic testing (HLA-DQ2/DQ8) isn't used to confirm the diagnosis — too many people carry the gene without disease — but it's useful to rule celiac disease out when the picture is ambiguous, since a negative result makes celiac disease very unlikely.

One practical note: all of this needs to happen while gluten is still being eaten regularly. Removing gluten before testing lets the antibody levels and the villi both start recovering, which can produce a false negative on tests taken afterward.

11 — The only treatment

Strict, lifelong gluten elimination — and why "strict" matters

There's no drug that treats celiac disease directly; the only established treatment is removing gluten from the diet completely.[5] Once gluten stops arriving, the whole cascade above has nothing to trigger it: antibody levels typically fall over months, and the villi themselves can regenerate — though full histological recovery can take one to two years, sometimes longer in adults than in children.

"Strict" isn't an exaggeration: because the immune trigger is a specific, potent peptide, even small amounts of cross-contamination (shared toasters, fryer oil, mislabeled products) are enough to re-trigger the immune cascade in a genuinely sensitive person, even without obvious symptoms each time. Repeated low-level exposure can keep the villi from ever fully healing.

12 — Left untreated

What years of unaddressed exposure adds up to

Bone & reproductive

Chronic calcium/vitamin D malabsorption raises osteoporosis and fracture risk; celiac disease is also linked to higher rates of infertility and adverse pregnancy outcomes.[5]

Refractory disease

A small subset of patients continue having damage despite a strict gluten-free diet, requiring specialist management.

Cancer risk

Long-standing untreated disease is associated with an increased risk of enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma and certain other GI cancers, though absolute risk is still low.[5]

Mortality

Population studies link prolonged, undiagnosed celiac disease to a modest increase in all-cause mortality, largely explained by the complications above.[5]
