in plain terms

The two-minute version

No jargon — the same story, told the way you'd tell a friend. The full science, molecule by molecule, is right below this if you want it.

Something stresses you out
a hard conversation, a deadline, sudden bad news
Your brain hits the alarm
it fires a signal down to your gut almost instantly
Your stomach makes extra acid
like it's bracing for a fight that never actually comes
Digestion gets put on hold
your body decides breaking down food can wait
Your gut says "let's clear out"
that sudden urge to go, right before something big

None of this means something is wrong with you — it's an old survival reflex reacting to a modern kind of stress. If it happens often enough to disrupt your day, that's worth a conversation with a doctor.

node 1 · brain

One stimulus, two speeds

A jolt, a spiral of anxious thought, or a medication acting on the same circuits — all converge on one brainstem hub, then split into a fast direct line and a slower hormonal one.

Stimulus
acute stress · anxiety · SSRI / benzodiazepine / TCA
Hypothalamus — PVN
CRH released
Pituitary → Adrenal
ACTH → cortisol · systemic, minutes–hours
Vagal nucleus (DMNX)
Vagus nerve → ACh · direct, seconds
Causal chain
Stimulus → PVN: CRH ↑
CRH → Pituitary: ACTH ↑ → Adrenal cortex: Cortisol ↑
CRH → DMNX → Vagus n.: ACh released

The vagal path reaches the gut before cortisol has even peaked — this is why a stomach can react before you've consciously registered "I'm anxious."

node 2 · stomach

Three signals, one pump

Nerve, hormone, and messenger chemical all end at the same doorstep: the proton pump on the parietal cell wall.

ACh
from vagus n. → M3 receptor
Gastrin
G cell → CCK2R on ECL cell → histamine
H⁺ / K⁺-ATPase
the proton pump, parietal cell apical membrane
HCl → gastric lumen
acid output rises
Causal reaction equations
ACh + M3R →(Gq)→ PLC → IP₃ + DAG → Ca²⁺ ↑ → PKC → pump ↑
Gastrin + CCK2R(ECL) →(Gq)→ Ca²⁺ ↑ → histamine released
Histamine + H2R →(Gs)→ cAMP ↑ → PKA → pump ↑
H⁺/K⁺-ATPase: ATP + K⁺(lumen) → ADP + Pᵢ + K⁺(cell) + H⁺(lumen)
CO₂ + H₂O →(carbonic anhydrase)→ H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻
H⁺ + Cl⁻ → HCl (gastric lumen)

Stress doesn't create acid from nothing — it turns up three receptors that are already there, all day, every day.

node 3 · small intestine

The quiet throttle-down

While the stomach is revving up, the same stress response is doing the opposite to your pancreas — cutting the enzyme supply that breaks food apart.

Sympathetic (NE)
α-adrenergic receptors, acinar cell
Splanchnic constriction
blood flow to gut ↓
Pancreatic secretion ↓
CCK-driven enzyme release blunted
Trypsinogen Chymotrypsinogen Lipase Amylase
Causal reaction equations
NE + α-AR(acinar cell) →(Gi)→ cAMP ↓ → enzyme exocytosis ↓
Sympathetic tone ↑ → splanchnic a. constriction → perfusion ↓ → secretory capacity ↓
CCK signalling blunted → trypsinogen, lipase, amylase output ↓
Trypsinogen →(enterokinase)→ Trypsin (self-amplifying activator)
Trypsin: protein–CO–NH–protein + H₂O → protein–COOH + H₂N–protein (peptide bond hydrolysis)
Lipase + colipase: triglyceride + H₂O → diglyceride + fatty acid (→ repeats to monoglyceride)

Less enzyme means less hydrolysis — larger, undigested fragments move on to the colon, which sets up what happens next.

node 4 · colon

The part everyone recognizes

CRH doesn't wait for cortisol. It acts directly on the colon wall — this is the molecular basis of the sudden, unmistakable urge.

CRH
direct colonic action, bypasses cortisol
CRH1 receptor
enterochromaffin cell + enteric neuron
Serotonin (5-HT) released
→ 5-HT3 receptor, vagal & spinal afferents
Peristaltic reflex triggered
Ascending
ACh + substance P → contraction (proximal)
Descending
NO + VIP → relaxation (distal)
Urgency
propulsive contraction · transit time ↓
Causal reaction equations
CRH + CRH1R(ECC, enteric neuron) → 5-HT release ↑
5-HT + 5-HT3R(afferents) → peristaltic reflex triggered
Ascending: ACh + substance P → circular muscle contraction (proximal)
Descending: NO + VIP → circular muscle relaxation (distal)
Net: transit time ↓, water-reabsorption window ↓ → urgency

Ninety percent of the body's serotonin is made in the gut, not the brain — which is why this reflex is so fast and so hard to override consciously.

where medication enters the circuit

Same wiring, three different taps

Antidepressants and anti-anxiety drugs don't add a new pathway — they turn dials on the ones above.

SSRIs

e.g. sertraline, fluoxetine

Raise synaptic 5-HT everywhere — including the gut, where enterochromaffin cells already hold most of the body's serotonin. Extra 5-HT3 / 5-HT4 activation early in treatment.

nausea / loose stool, often early & transient
Benzodiazepines

e.g. lorazepam, alprazolam

GABA-A agonism centrally dampens hypothalamic and vagal outflow — often quiets both the acid surge and the urgency reflex at the source.

tends to normalize, chronic use can slow transit
TCAs

e.g. amitriptyline

Anticholinergic — blocks the same M3 receptors that ACh uses to trigger acid and motility. Turns both dials down together.

constipation is the dominant GI effect
