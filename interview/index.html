Right now these answers are accurate, structured, and safe. Below is the rewrite for every answer, the four priority fixes, and the one physical artifact that will do more than all the words combined.

01

Four priority fixes, do these first

  1. Rewrite the Q1 opener. Lead with outcome, not architecture. Nobody feels anything when you say "closed-loop system" in sentence one.

  2. Bring the Swapna endoscopy report into the room as a physical document. Produce it when answering the proof question. That single moment is worth more than all the words combined.

02

Round one, full question by question

R1 / Q1
What exactly is Orcasho, and why does the world need it now?
Needs rewrite

Opens with a definition, not a problem. Nobody feels anything at "closed-loop system" in sentence one. The 96% PPI number is buried at the end when it should open the room. Architecture before outcome is the wrong sequence for a pitch room.

original answer
Orcasho is a closed-loop system that stabilizes digestion and absorption so patients respond better to food and medication. Today, chronic care is episodic, built around one-time interventions while the patient's body keeps changing every day...
rewritten answer, use this

Every chronic patient you will ever meet is being managed as if their body is static. It is not. Their biology changes every day based on what they eat, what they absorb, and how their gut environment interacts with the medications they are already taking. Current care doesn't track that change. It diagnoses, prescribes, and then waits for the next visit.

We built a system that tracks it continuously and adjusts every 20 days. The result: 96% of our patients have discontinued PPIs. Not because we told them to. Because their body stabilized enough that it no longer needed acid suppression.

That outcome, biological stabilization through continuous adaptation, is what chronic care has never been able to produce before. We use digestion and absorption as the control point because it sits upstream of almost every chronic symptom. Stabilize that layer and the downstream burden reduces on its own. Patients stay with us 9 to 18 months, which means we accumulate longitudinal feedback that makes the system more accurate over time. That is the compounding advantage no episodic care model can replicate.

why this version is stronger
  • Opens with a problem the interviewer feels immediately.
  • Places the 96% PPI outcome in the first paragraph where it creates impact.
  • Explains the mechanism only after the outcome has landed emotionally.
  • Ends with the compounding advantage, which seeds the venture-scale conversation.
R1 / Q2
Why can't existing healthcare systems or large companies already solve this?
Needs one key reframe

Accurate but abstract. Tells Permanente interviewers their system is episodic. They already know this and live with it daily, so it risks making them feel criticized rather than understood.

rewritten answer, use this

Permanente is exceptional at the clinical layer. Diagnosis, treatment, specialist referral, acute intervention. That is exactly what it is designed for and what it does well.

What exists nowhere, not in Permanente, not in any health system globally, is what happens to that chronic patient in the 340 days of the year they are not in a clinical setting. Their gut environment is changing every day. Their medication response is shifting. Their absorption is varying with every meal. Nobody is tracking that. Nobody is adjusting for it. The patient comes back six months later and the physician sees the outcome of 180 days of unmonitored biological drift with no data on what caused it.

We fill that gap. We track every 20 days. We adjust based on what we find. And we feed that data back to the physician in a dashboard they can check any time.

This cannot be built inside a clinical system because clinical systems are structured around episodic encounters, and they are not designed or incentivized to run continuous adaptation cycles between visits. It has to be built outside and integrated in. That is exactly what we do.

why this version is stronger
  • Opens by complimenting Permanente's strength, not criticizing their gap.
  • Makes the gap feel concrete with the "340 days" framing.
  • Positions Orcasho as filling the gap rather than replacing the system.
  • Ends by explaining why the integration model is necessary, not just different.
R1 / Q3
Why are you the right person to build this?
Needs one irreplaceable sentence

Too humble and too process-oriented. Describes what you did but not why only you could have seen it. Reads like a CV summary rather than a founder origin story.

rewritten answer, use this

Most people looked at gut dysfunction and saw a symptom to suppress. I looked at it and saw a control variable.

That reframe, from symptom to control variable, is what made it possible to design a feedback loop instead of a protocol. It came from fundamental chemistry training, and it is not an obvious move from inside medicine. Clinicians are trained to identify and treat. I was trained to identify and model. Those are different instincts that produce different architectures.

From that reframe, everything else followed. We modeled each patient's internal state as a 15-dimensional biochemical vector. We selected interventions based on ionic directional alignment using cosine similarity. We built adaptation into the core of the system rather than treating it as an exception.

I could only build this because I didn't approach it as a healthcare problem. I approached it as a control-system problem that happened to involve human biology. That specific combination of chemistry background and control-system thinking is what produced the architecture that is now running across 300 patients.

why this version is stronger
  • Opens with the single most irreplaceable sentence in the entire interview.
  • Makes the scientific uniqueness concrete and immediately understandable.
  • Shows depth of thinking without academic jargon.
  • Ends with a proof point, 300 patients, that grounds the founder story in reality.
R1 / Q4
What is the single biggest thing you've learned from your users?
Strong, one addition

Actually the strongest Round 1 answer already. The stability-vs-improvement insight is genuinely non-obvious. Only missing a concrete patient moment before the analytical point.

rewritten answer, use this

We had a patient, post-chemotherapy, on Tamoxifen, who came to us because her digestion was completely destabilized after treatment. After three cycles she told us something that changed how we think about what we are building.

She said: "I'm not excited because I feel better. I'm excited because I feel the same way two days in a row for the first time in three years."

That sentence is the clearest articulation of what we learned. Patients don't struggle because they lack treatment. They struggle because their response is inconsistent. When digestion stabilizes, the consistency spreads. They don't just improve symptoms, they experience predictability in how their body reacts to food, to medication, to daily variation.

That shift from improvement to stability is what drives long-term retention. And it is what traditional episodic care never measures, because it is only visible across time, not in a single clinical encounter.

Round two, full question by question

R2 / Q1
How do you know this isn't just correlation?
The hardest question in the interview

The current answer describes process, not the epistemological challenge. A Permanente clinician hears "repeated observation" and thinks: that is not causal evidence, that is sophisticated anecdote. The fix is to acknowledge the limitation directly, then explain why the evidence design is still meaningful.

rewritten answer, use this

You are right that we do not have a randomized controlled trial. I want to be direct about that.

What we have is different, and in some ways more relevant to chronic care management than a standard RCT. We run repeated within-patient intervention cycles. In each cycle, we change one or two variables in a patient's protocol and observe exactly how their biological system responds. When the same protocol change produces the same directional response across multiple cycles in the same patient, and then we see that pattern replicated across patients with similar biochemical profiles, that convergence is how we identify causal signal rather than natural variation.

This is closer to a series of n-of-1 trials repeated across 300 patients than to a population-level RCT. It does not give us population-level statistical significance. It gives us mechanistic specificity at the individual patient level across repeated intervention cycles.

Then there is the endoscopy evidence.

produce the swapna report here

A before-and-after UGI endoscopy from two independent hospitals, 8 months apart. Before: hyperemic OG junction with GERD Grade A, fundal erosions, duodenitis, pyloric edema. After: esophagus normal, fundus normal, duodenum normal, pylorus normal. Objective tissue-level documentation from a gastroenterologist with no relationship to the company. That is not correlation.

closing the answer

We know a formal trial is the right next step and we are building the data infrastructure to support it. But the combination of repeated within-patient intervention cycles and independent clinical documentation is not nothing. It is the strongest real-world evidence base you can build without institutional research funding.

the critical move in this answer
  • The moment you say "let me show you what this looks like" and physically place the document on the table, the energy of the room shifts.
  • Words describe a system. An independent gastroenterologist's report showing healed tissue proves it. Do not skip this moment.
R2 / Q2
What exactly becomes harder to replicate as you scale?
Strong, one addition

The strongest Round 2 answer already. The intervention-response pattern framing is correct. Missing one addition: depth per patient versus number of patients, the distinction that makes the moat argument genuinely defensible.

rewritten answer, use this

What becomes hard to replicate is not the data volume. It is the intervention-response depth built over repeated cycles per patient.

A competitor who launches tomorrow and signs up 10,000 patients gets cross-sectional data. They know what 10,000 people look like at one point in time. We have patients who have been through 15 to 20 intervention cycles over 18 months. We know how those specific patients respond when we change specific variables. We know what happens in cycle 4 when cycle 3 produced a particular response pattern.

That longitudinal depth per patient cannot be acquired by signing up more patients faster. It can only be accumulated through time, one 20-day cycle at a time. A well-funded competitor starting today would need 18 months minimum to get their first patients to the data depth our current patients already represent. And by then, our existing patients will have added another 18 months of cycles.

The moat is not data. The moat is time. You cannot compress 20-day biological response cycles. Biology does not run faster with more compute.

R2 / Q3
What happens if a patient gets worse under your system?
Good, one addition needed

Solid framing already, escalation protocol and working alongside healthcare is correct. Missing the safety record itself. Permanente is thinking about liability, give them the answer before they ask.

rewritten answer, use this

First, the record: we have not had a serious adverse event across 300 patients over multiple years of operation. That is not luck. That is design.

We work exclusively with known supplement-grade compounds from a vetted library of 70 to 100 components. We make incremental adjustments every 20 days rather than large sudden protocol changes. Every change is directionally validated against the patient's current biochemical state before it is applied. The risk profile of what we do is categorically different from pharmaceutical intervention.

That said, if a patient is worsening, our 20-day feedback cycle is specifically designed for early detection. We track changes in symptoms and response patterns so we can identify biological instability before it becomes a clinical crisis. When we see those signals, we adjust conservatively based on prior patterns in that specific patient.

More importantly, we escalate. We are not a replacement for clinical care, we are an extension of it. If there are signs that require medical evaluation, we direct the patient back to their physician immediately and use that clinical input to refine the next cycle. In the Permanente model, the physician dashboard means that escalation is visible to the clinical team in real time. They can intervene before we even need to flag it.

R2 / Q4
Why is this a venture-scale company and not just a niche service?
Needs the data-compounding argument

Good on operational leverage numbers, 7 to 8 minutes per patient, 1,000 patients per operator is impressive. But those numbers describe a services business with good margins. The data-compounding argument, the actually stronger case, is completely missing.

rewritten answer, use this

There are two layers to the venture-scale case, and most people only hear the first one.

The first layer is operational leverage. We have reduced human intervention time from 30 minutes per patient per cycle to 7 to 8 minutes per month. One operator manages over 1,000 patients. 90% of decisions are automated. Patient growth does not require proportional headcount growth. That is good unit economics, but alone it describes a services business with strong margins.

The second layer is where the venture-scale case actually lives. Every patient cycle we run adds to a proprietary dataset that makes the next intervention more accurate. This is not just scaling capacity. The system is scaling intelligence.

At 300 patients we have a working system. At 3,000 patients we have a pattern-recognition layer. At 30,000 patients we have a predictive infrastructure that no competitor can build from scratch, because they would need the same years of longitudinal intervention-response cycles to generate it.

That is when the data layer becomes licensable. To other health systems. To pharma companies that want to understand real-world supplement-drug interaction patterns. To insurance companies that want to predict chronic care cost trajectories.

We do not become venture scale by running more patients through the same system. We become venture scale when the accumulated intelligence from those patients creates a data layer with value far beyond any single patient interaction. That transition happens somewhere between 5,000 and 15,000 patients. We are building toward it deliberately.
