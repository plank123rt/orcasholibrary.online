00 · Why bother with this test?

Blood tells you today. Nerves tell you the last few years.

A blood test shows what's happening in the blood right now. Nerve damage builds up silently, over months or years, long before symptoms are noticeable a nerve conduction study is how that hidden history gets read.

Your nerve, healthy

Signal flows smoothly and reaches every part of the body no symptoms, nothing to feel.

Rusting quietly

Sugar and cholesterol act like rust the pipe narrows from the inside. Little damage is felt yet.

Narrowed & blocked

Less signal reaches the far end. This is usually when symptoms tingling, numbness, weakness finally show up.

Your nerve is that pipe. Inside it, an electrical signal is meant to flow smoothly to every part of the body it serves. By the time symptoms appear, the "rusting" has usually been going on quietly for a while which is exactly what this study is built to detect early.
01 · The idea

Stimulate. Travel. Arrive. Record.

A small electrical pulse is given over a nerve. A recording electrode downstream catches what arrives and turns it into a trace on screen.

01

Stimulate

A brief pulse is applied over the nerve, at a known point and known time.

02

Propagate

The impulse runs along the nerve fibre toward the recording site.

03

Arrive

It reaches a muscle (motor study) or a nearby patch of skin (sensory study).

04

Record

A waveform is drawn its shape holds the timing and strength of the signal.

02 · The vocabulary

Four numbers tell the whole story

Latency

Time from stimulus to the first response mostly reflects the fastest, most heavily myelinated fibres.

↑ prolonged → myelin slowing

Amplitude

Size of the response a direct headcount of how many axons (or muscle fibres) actually fired.

↓ reduced → axon loss

Conduction Velocity

Distance ÷ time between two stimulation points the true speed of transmission along that segment.

↓ slowed → demyelination

F‑wave

A signal that runs up to the spinal cord and bounces back checks the nerve's proximal segment, close to the root.

↑ latency → proximal disease

04 · Motor conduction

Stimulate the nerve, record the muscle

Each nerve is stimulated at two points; the muscle's response (CMAP) is recorded at both, so speed over that segment can be calculated.

Median nerve · motor

Slowed focal demyelinating pattern
LEFT · wrist→APBDML 4.25 ms
RIGHT · wrist→APBDML 4.38 ms
CV L 46.0 m/sCV R 48.4 m/s CMAP amp 10.9 / 13.4 mV (normal)ref CV ≥ 50 m/s

Latency is prolonged and velocity slowed only across the wrist while the strength of the signal (amplitude) is untouched. That combination slow but full‑sized is the signature of a squeezed nerve losing its insulation at one spot, not of dying fibres.

Ulnar & radial nerves · motor

Normal, both sides
Ulnar · wrist→ADMCV 50.5 m/s
Radial · below spiralCV 57–64 m/s

These neighbouring nerves in the same limbs conduct normally an important control. It tells us the problem at the median nerve is local, not a generalised arm‑wide process.

Reduced signal axonal pattern
LEFT · ankle→EDBAmp 2.38 mV
RIGHT · ankle→EDBAmp 1.91 mV

Here the wave never grows tall the muscle response is small because fewer axons are delivering the signal at all. This is the nerve that travels furthest down the leg, and it's the one showing the most wear.

Tibial nerve · motor

Normal, both sides
LEFT · ankle→EHLCV 42.8 m/s
RIGHT · ankle→EHLCV 43.3 m/s

A second leg nerve, unaffected narrowing the picture to specific fibre types rather than every nerve below the knee.

F‑waves · all tested nerves

Normal latency

The round‑trip signal to the spinal cord and back returned on time everywhere it was checked arguing against extra slowing along the proximal segment or nerve root.

05 · Sensory conduction

Stimulate the nerve, record the skin

No muscle involved here the recorded signal (SNAP) is the sensory fibres alone, and it's naturally far smaller (µV, not mV).

LEFTCV 35.4 m/s
RIGHTCV 37.3 m/s

Same story as the motor fibres of this nerve: the sensory branch crossing the wrist is slow, but full amplitude. Both point at the same spot the carpal tunnel.

Speed here is actually fine it's purely the size of the signal that's fallen. Small, on‑time responses at the ankle and calf, the body's most distant sensory territory, is the classic fingerprint of a length‑dependent axonal process.

06 · Two mechanisms

Slow & strong vs. fast & faint

Every abnormal nerve in this study falls into one of two mechanical stories.

Demyelinating

The myelin insulation thins or breaks down at one point. The signal survives but leaks and slows like water pressure dropping through a damaged pipe.

Latency ↑ up
Amplitude normal
Velocity ↓ down
Seen here in median nerve
Your resultMild demyelinating neuropathy in both median nerves (hands) mild slowing of signals.

Axonal

The nerve fibre itself degenerates, usually starting at its farthest tip. Fewer wires are left carrying current the signal that does arrive is on time, just smaller.

Latency normal
Amplitude ↓ down
Velocity normal / mild ↓
Seen here in peroneal & sural nerves
Your resultAxonal neuropathy in both peroneal and sural nerves (legs) some nerve fibres are actually damaged.
07 · Putting it together

Two patterns, two diagnoses

Finding

Median nerve slows sharply only across the wrist normal everywhere else, normal in neighbouring nerves.

Reading

A focal entrapment at a predictable chokepoint → bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome.

Finding

Peroneal & sural nerves the longest nerves in the body show reduced signal size, on both sides.

Reading

A distal, symmetric, length‑dependent process → axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy, fitting the diabetic‑neuropathy question raised at referral.

Final impression

1) Bilateral median motor‑sensory mild demyelinating neuropathy (entrapment at the wrist).
2) Bilateral common peroneal & sural motor‑sensory axonal neuropathy.
To be correlated clinically as with any electrodiagnostic report.

08 · The bigger toolkit

What else this test can uncover

Nerve conduction studies (often paired with needle EMG) are used far beyond this one case:

Entrapment neuropathies

Pinpointing exactly where a nerve is compressed carpal tunnel, ulnar nerve at the elbow, peroneal nerve at the fibular head.

Diffuse polyneuropathies

Diabetes, vitamin B12 deficiency, alcohol, CIDP, Guillain‑Barré, and inherited types like Charcot‑Marie‑Tooth each leave a distinct fingerprint.

Nerve root problems

F‑waves and H‑reflexes probe the segment closest to the spinal cord useful support when a radiculopathy is suspected.

Neuromuscular junction disease

Repetitive nerve stimulation reveals the decrementing response typical of myasthenia gravis or Lambert‑Eaton syndrome.

Injury severity & recovery

Serial studies after trauma or nerve surgery track whether regrowth is actually happening, and how completely.

Demyelinating vs. axonal

This single distinction made throughout this report shapes both the likely cause and the treatment path.
